The savings rate sets the retirement date before returns do

A target retirement number comes from one multiplication: annual spending times 25, the 4% rule. The date attached to that number comes from a division, and the savings rate is the divisor. Strip market returns out and the clock is exact: 25 times annual spending, divided by annual saving.

Run a $60,000 salary through it. The 10 percent saver banks $6,000 a year and spends the other $54,000, so the target is $1,350,000 and the clock reads 225 years. 1,350,000 divided by 6,000 is 225, which is better than five full 40-year careers spent earning the date.

Push the rate to 50 percent and the same salary banks $30,000, spends $30,000, and needs $750,000: 25 years, one career long. Between those rows the curve is steep at the bottom. Moving from 10 to 20 percent, a $500 monthly spending cut, drops the clock from 225 years to 100 and removes 125 of them. The identical $500 cut from 40 to 50 percent removes 12.5. The first ten points are worth ten times the last ten.

Market returns shrink every row. At a 5 percent real return the 10 percent clock drops to about 51 years and the 50 percent clock to about 17. The shape survives the compounding: the bottom of the range stays where the years are cheapest to buy.

The fraction explains the steepness. A dollar of spending cut works both ends of the expression at once: the target drops by 25 times the dollar and yearly saving rises by the same dollar. A saved raise moves one end. That is why the rate is the number FIRE planning quotes first, before any salary figure.

The division sets the date; the withdrawal math decides whether the date holds. FireNomics works that second half: sequence-of-returns risk, guru-course expected value, and where the 4% rule breaks on 50-year horizons.
